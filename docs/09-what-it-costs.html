What it costs

This program costs $5,178 a year. The cheapest schooling actually available to this family costs $20,163. The school on the installation ten minutes away costs $26,676, and the US government calculated that figure itself.

Every number below traces to an invoice, a published tuition memorandum, or a named federal statistic. Where a figure is an estimate, it says so.

"Free" is the wrong word

Most cost comparisons put public school at zero. That is not true in any useful sense — it is simply a bill paid somewhere the family does not see it. A public school seat costs real money, somebody pays for it, and the people paying include the family, through taxes, whether or not their child ever sits in it.

So this page reports what a year of school costs, and states who pays rather than pretending the question away.

For this family the distinction collapses anyway, because none of the options are free to us. That turns out to be the most useful thing we have to report.

That is $431.51 a month, spread across the year.

Two of those deserve a sentence.

Scouting is genuinely free, and not because anyone waived a fee. The troop sells cookies, and the proceeds cover the annual membership. It is the only line on this page that costs nothing, and it costs nothing because of work the kids did.

$200 a year to mail homework is what being overseas adds. An accredited program requires original graded papers to reach the school four times a year by a fixed date. From an APO that is an overnight international shipment each time. A family in Ohio pays for stamps.

Abeka ships materials to us at no charge, so the shipping cost runs one direction only.

What is not counted: paper, binders and toner beyond the ink line; the printer itself, which the household already owned and also uses for a business. Counting the full ink cost against school rather than splitting it with the business overstates our cost slightly. That is the safe direction to be wrong in on a page like this, so we left it that way.

Monthly figures are the annual cost spread across twelve months, which is how a household actually feels it. The schools themselves bill differently — ICSP monthly over the school year, the DoD school in two payments tied to the federal fiscal year — so these are for comparison, not a payment schedule.

The Department of War Education Activity rate

The DoD school system — renamed the Department of War Education Activity, still widely known as DoDEA — publishes a tuition rate each year for students who are not entitled to attend free. For School Year 2026–2027, in Europe, the Pacific and Cuba, full-day pre-K and grades 1–6:

Annual Semester Quarter
Federally connected $26,676.00 $13,338.00 $6,669.00
Non-federally connected $27,600.00 $13,800.00 $6,900.00

The memorandum, signed by the DoWEA Comptroller on 5 May 2026, explains how the number is built:

Tuition charges include a non-refundable Treasury fee. Both the Treasury fee and tuition rates are calculated using a full cost recovery methodology in compliance with Government Accountability Office (GAO) guidance.

That sentence is why this page leads with the figure. $26,676 is not a market price or an advocacy estimate. It is the US government's own full-cost calculation of one elementary school seat for one year, computed to GAO standards and signed by a comptroller. The same memorandum describes tuition as "a debt owed to the U.S. Government."

Anyone who says public school is free is welcome to read it.

Why that rate applies to us

Tuition-free enrollment in the DoD schools is for command-sponsored dependents of active-duty members and DoD civilians. This household is headed by a separated veteran, honorably discharged — not active duty, not a DoD civilian, not command sponsored.

When this family asked the Osan Elementary registrar about enrollment, the reply asked for the documents needed to request a "Space Available — tuition paying student," and listed among them "a copy of your base pass showing your veteran's status." The classification was not hypothetical. It was made, in writing, by the school.

The two rates differ by whether a family is "federally connected." Which category applies to a separated veteran is DoWEA's determination, not ours, so both are printed above.

What this page does not show

It does not show that homeschooling is cheap. The average American homeschooling family spends about $1,295 a year on an elementary student, according to a 2024 survey of more than 4,000 families by the Home School Legal Defense Association. This program costs four times that. We buy an accredited enrollment with graded transcripts, and we pay a tutoring academy three days a week. A family doing this on library books and free curriculum would spend a fraction of what we spend.

So the claim is not that homeschooling saves money. It is narrower and harder to argue with: we spend well above what the average homeschooling family spends, and it still costs a fifth of the school down the road.

It does not count a parent's time. The largest real cost of homeschooling is the income a parent does not earn while teaching. It is absent from every line above, it is absent from most published homeschool cost figures, and it is the single biggest reason a cost comparison like this one can mislead. A family weighing this decision should put their own number on it.

It is one family, one year, one country. Costs here are shaped by being American, overseas, and not entitled to a DoD school seat. A family in Ohio faces a completely different set of options, most of which are genuinely free at the point of use.

Currency. KRW figures are converted at 1 USD = 1,335.68 KRW, the rate on 16 November 2026. The won moves; the conversions do not update themselves.
