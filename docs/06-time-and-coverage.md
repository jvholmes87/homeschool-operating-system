# 06 — Time and coverage, measured against a public elementary benchmark

This is a **time and coverage** comparison. It makes no claim about learning outcomes;
see `07-claims-and-limits.md` for why not.

**Core instruction hours are measured.** The daily packet logs start time, finish time and
total breaks, and the tracker holds every graded result.

**Enrichment hours are estimated** and labelled as such throughout. They are the
programme owner's figures for session lengths, not logged times. Treat the core column as
evidence and the enrichment column as a good-faith estimate until it is logged the same way.

---

## The benchmark

US public elementary, grades 1–4, from the federal Schools and Staffing Survey:

| Subject | Hours per week |
|---|---|
| English / language arts | 11.6 |
| Mathematics | 5.4 |
| Social studies | 2.5 |
| Science | 2.3 |
| **Core instruction** | **21.8** |
| Student school week | 32.1 |
| **Not core instruction** | **10.3 (32% of the week)** |

Nationally the average student spends about **1,231 hours in school per year**, and the
same body of research finds interruptions and teacher absence cost a meaningful slice of
that — roughly 97 hours a year in one district study.

The number that matters is the third row: **about a third of the public elementary week is
not core instruction.** Transitions, lining up, attendance, assemblies, and the overhead of
moving 25 children through a building. None of it is wasted exactly, but none of it is
instruction either.

## This programme

Measured across Days 17–22 of the first grading period:

| | Hours |
|---|---|
| Working time per school day | 3.9 – 5.3 (mid 4.6) |
| Per week, 5 school days | **19.6 – 26.7 (mid 23.1)** |

**Important caveat for honest comparison:** those hours cover **eight** subjects — Bible,
Reading, Language, Writing, Spelling, Arithmetic, History and Science — where the 21.8-hour
benchmark covers four. Spelling and handwriting are separately timetabled here and are
folded into English in the benchmark. The totals are close; the baskets are not identical.
Read it as *comparable*, not *equal*.

## Enrichment

Structured, scheduled activity outside the core programme. Reported **as hours by category**
— not as days, times, named organisations or locations, because this is a public document
about a specific child.

*Estimated, not logged — see the note at the top.*

| Category | Hours per week (est.) |
|---|---|
| Academic tutoring (mathematics and a second language), 3×/week | 9 – 12 |
| Scouting, one meeting per week | 1.5 – 2 |
| Sports club | 1.5 – 2 |
| Youth sports — 2 practices + 1 game per week, seasonal | 3 – 4.5 |
| **Total** | **15 – 20.5** |

Of that, roughly 10 hours is additional academic instruction and roughly 7 hours is
structured time with peers.

## The comparison

| | Hours per week |
|---|---|
| Public elementary, in school | 32.1 — of which 21.8 is core instruction |
| This programme, core instruction | 19.6 – 26.7 |
| This programme, core + enrichment | **34.6 – 47.2 structured** |

**Core instruction is comparable.** The difference is what fills the rest of the week:
about 10 hours of non-instructional school time, against 15–20 hours of chosen academic
tutoring, scouting and sport.

That is the whole claim. Not better learning — **the same instructional load in a shorter
day, with the recovered time spent on things that were chosen rather than inherited.**

## On the socialization question

The most common objection to homeschooling is social isolation. The research does not
support the objection in either direction — reviewers find no evidence of help or harm, and
say so because the studies are too weak to conclude anything.

What this programme can report is a schedule: **roughly 7 hours a week of structured peer
activity across scouting and two sports programmes, plus 3 sessions a week in a classroom
with other children.** Five days a week this student is in a room with other kids doing
something organised.

That is not evidence about socialization outcomes. It is evidence that the *scheduling*
objection — that a homeschooled child has no structured peer contact — does not describe
this programme. Those are different claims and the weaker one is the defensible one.

## Flexibility, concretely

The things the schedule can absorb that a fixed timetable cannot:

- **A subject can run at the student's actual level.** Mathematics here runs well above
  grade level because there is nothing forcing it to the median of a class.
- **Work can move sites.** The mathematics work-text is done at a tutoring centre three days
  a week; the home packets carry only the video lessons and the graded instruments. The
  split is documented so neither site duplicates or drops the other's work.
- **A bad day can be re-dated instead of lost.** When a grading period slipped two weeks, it
  was re-baselined and brought back inside its deadline by adding one weekend make-up day —
  not by cutting content.
- **Assessment is placed where the student is fresh.** Graded items are deliberately
  positioned in the packet. One quiz taken early, out of its planned slot, produced a score
  10 points below the same student's rate-matched result a week earlier — visible precisely
  because the conditions were logged.

The last one cuts both ways and is included for that reason.
