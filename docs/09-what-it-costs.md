# What it costs

Our homeschool costs **$5,178 a year**. The cheapest schooling actually available
to this family costs **$20,163**. The school on the installation ten minutes away
costs **$26,676**, and the US government calculated that figure itself.

Every number here came off an invoice, a signed tuition memorandum, or a federal
statistics table. Where a figure is a guess, it says so.

{{chart:cost-year}}

---

## "Free" is the wrong word

Most cost comparisons put public school at zero.

Public school is not free. It costs about $16,560 a year per student. You pay for
it in property tax and income tax, every year, whether or not your child ever
sits in one of those seats. The bill just doesn't arrive with your name on it.

So this page does two things: it says what a year of school costs, and it says
who writes the check.

For us the question is simpler than that. Nobody is offering us a free seat. The
school on the installation sent us a price.

## What we pay

| Line | Year | Source |
|---|---|---|
| Abeka Grade 4 Video & Books, Accredited enrollment | $1,454.90 | Abeka invoice, 10 Aug 2026 |
| Tutoring academy — math and Korean, 3 days/week | $1,707.00 | KRW 190,000/month × 12 |
| Volleyball club, off base | $1,581.22 | KRW 176,000/month × 12 |
| Shipping graded work to the school | $200.00 | FedEx overnight, $50 × 4 grading periods |
| Printer ink | $150.00 | $30/set, replaced every 2–3 months |
| Youth volleyball, on base | $85.00 | Youth Programs account statement |
| Scouting | **$0.00** | Annual dues covered by troop cookie sales |
| **Total** | **$5,178.11** | |

That is **$431.51 a month**, spread across the year.

Two of those deserve a sentence.

**Scouting is genuinely free**, and not because anyone waived a fee. The troop
sells cookies, and the proceeds cover the annual membership. It is the only line
on this page that costs nothing, and it costs nothing because of work the kids
did.

**$200 a year to mail homework.** Four times a year the original graded papers
have to reach Pensacola by a deadline. From an APO box that means FedEx
overnight, $50 a shipment. A family in Ohio buys a stamp.

Abeka ships materials *to* us at no charge, so the shipping cost runs one
direction only.

**Not counted:** paper, binders, and the printer itself, which was already in
the house and also prints invoices for a business.
Counting the full ink cost against school rather than splitting it with the
business overstates our cost slightly. That is the safe direction to be wrong in
on a page like this, so we left it that way.

Monthly figures are the annual cost spread across twelve months, which is how a
household actually feels it. The schools themselves bill differently — ICSP
monthly over the school year, the DoD school in two payments tied to the federal
fiscal year — so these are for comparison, not a payment schedule.

### Why Austin ISD is in that table twice

Austin is not a school we could send her to. It is in the table because it is
the rare district that publishes its whole ledger, so you can see something the
national average hides: how much of a school budget is school.

In 2024-25 Austin ISD spent **$38,486 per student**. Two things happen to that
money before any of it reaches a teacher.

About **$10,907 per student leaves Texas's second-largest city entirely**. Texas
recaptures property tax revenue from districts the state considers wealthy and
redistributes it. Austin is the largest payer in the state — roughly $777
million in FY2025, more than 40% of its revenue, more than the next several
districts combined. The district never gets to spend it on anyone in Austin.
Take it out and Austin spends **$27,580 per student**, which is the row above.

Another **$11,992 per student is buildings and debt** — construction, and paying
off the bonds that financed earlier construction. In Texas that comes from a
separate tax rate voters approve on its own.

What is left for instruction and instruction-related costs is **$8,678 per
student**.

{{chart:austin-split}}

That $8,678 is a narrow definition. It is a budget function code, and it
excludes counselors, nurses, librarians, buses and lunch, which most parents
would call part of school. The two honest numbers are both in the table above:
$27,580 is what the district spends, $8,678 is what reaches the classroom, and
our $5,178 sits between them.

We are not claiming the three buy the same thing. We are claiming that only one
of the three publishes what it costs per child without being asked.

### The Department of War Education Activity rate

Every year the DoD school system — renamed the Department of War Education
Activity, still called DoDEA by everyone — publishes what it charges a student
who is not entitled to a free seat. For 2026–2027, in Europe, the Pacific and
Cuba, **full-day pre-K through grade 6**:

| | Annual | Semester | Quarter |
|---|---|---|---|
| Federally connected | $26,676.00 | $13,338.00 | $6,669.00 |
| Non-federally connected | $27,600.00 | $13,800.00 | $6,900.00 |

The memorandum, signed by the DoWEA Comptroller on 5 May 2026, explains how the
number is built:

> Tuition charges include a non-refundable Treasury fee. Both the Treasury fee
> and tuition rates are calculated using a full cost recovery methodology in
> compliance with Government Accountability Office (GAO) guidance.

Read that again. The government worked out what one seat in that school costs
to run for a year, checked the arithmetic against GAO rules, and a comptroller
signed her name to it.

**$26,676.**

Nobody is selling anything. That is the cost, on federal letterhead. The same
memo calls unpaid tuition "a debt owed to the U.S. Government," which is a
reminder that somebody always pays — usually you, through taxes, for a seat your
child may never use.

### Why that rate applies to us

Tuition-free enrollment in the DoD schools is for command-sponsored dependents of
active-duty members and DoD civilians. This household is headed by a **separated
veteran**, honorably discharged — not active duty, not a DoD civilian, not
command sponsored.

When this family asked the Osan Elementary registrar about enrollment, the reply
asked for the documents needed to request a "**Space Available — tuition paying
student**," and listed among them "a copy of your base pass **showing your
veteran's status**." The classification was not hypothetical. It was made, in
writing, by the school.

The two rates differ by whether a family is "federally connected." Which
category applies to a separated veteran is DoWEA's determination, not ours, so
both are printed above.

### Microschools and learning pods

A microschool is the thing our homeschool would become if four more families
joined it: a small group, a rented room, one adult running the day, kids working
through their own coursework.

The National Microschooling Center surveyed 800 of them across all fifty states
and DC. What they charge:

| Annual tuition | Share of microschools |
|---|---|
| Under $5,000 | 26% |
| $5,000 – $10,000 | 48% |
| $10,000 – $15,000 | 16% |
| $15,000 – $20,000 | 5% |
| Over $20,000 | 5% |

Three quarters charge $10,000 or less, and about 65% offer sliding-scale tuition
or discounts. The median cost of educating one child is **$6,500**; the average
total cost per student is **$8,239**.

So a microschool costs roughly **60% more** than our homeschool.

The reason is the whole reason, and it is worth saying plainly: **a microschool
pays an adult.** Our $5,178 buys materials, tutoring and shipping. It does not
buy anybody's time, because the time is mine and I do not invoice for it. Put a
salary in and the gap closes immediately.

That is the same forgone-income point made at the bottom of this page, in a form
you can price. A microschool is what our homeschool costs when somebody has to be
paid to run it.

## Where the money actually goes

A fair question about any cost comparison is whether the models are even buying
the same thing. They are not.

{{chart:cost-breakdown}}

| | Our spend | Share |
|---|---|---|
| Tutoring academy — math and Korean | $1,707 | 33% |
| Sports and scouting | $1,666 | 32% |
| Curriculum and accredited enrollment | $1,455 | 28% |
| Shipping and printing | $350 | 7% |

**Curriculum is 28% of our budget.** The other 72% buys a specialist teaching
math three days a week, two volleyball programs, and the postage to get graded
work to Florida.

Which answers the obvious question about the $1,295 national average. That
figure, from HSLDA's 2024 survey of more than 4,000 families, is not a
curriculum-only number — the survey asked households to total curriculum,
supplies, co-op fees, online class tuition, transportation, lessons, sports and
tutoring in a single estimate. Same categories we track.

So the comparison is honest, and the finding is this: **our curriculum line
alone, $1,455, is larger than the average family's entire education budget.**
We spend four times the average because we buy an accredited enrollment with
third-party grading, and because we pay for enrichment most families either do
without or get free through a co-op.

A microschool inverts the ratio again. Microschools report spending a median of
$6,500 to educate one child, of which **curriculum is a few hundred dollars** —
about two in five spend at least $600 per student on curricular materials, and
most spend less. The remaining nine tenths is staff, space and food.

Stack the three up and a pattern falls out:

| | Total | Of which curriculum | Mostly buys |
|---|---|---|---|
| Average US homeschool | $1,295 | most of it | materials |
| **Our homeschool** | **$5,178** | **$1,455 (28%)** | **services and instruction** |
| US microschool | $8,239 | a few hundred | adults and a building |

**The more a model costs, the less of it is curriculum.** Teaching material is
cheap and has been for years. What costs money is people's time and the room
they stand in. We pay for part of that — a tutor three days a week — and absorb
the rest by not paying ourselves.

## What tax relief is available

Not tax advice, and worth taking to someone who files your return. The figures
below are what the statutes and published rates say.

**Federally: nothing.** Ordinary homeschool expenses are not deductible on a
federal return. Teaching your own child is treated as a family choice, not a
business expense or a charitable act.

**Louisiana is the exception, and this is a Louisiana household** — New Orleans
resident, filing a Louisiana return. Under R.S. 47:297.11 the state allows a
deduction of 50% of qualified educational expenses per dependent, capped since
2024 at $6,000 of deduction per child. Note what that is: a deduction from
taxable income, not a credit. The qualifying categories include curriculum,
supplies, tutoring and educational technology, so most of what is listed at the
top of this page counts.

Louisiana now has a flat 3% income tax, which makes the arithmetic short:

| | Amount |
|---|---|
| Our qualifying spend | $5,178 |
| Deduction at 50% | $2,589 |
| Tax saved at 3% | **about $78** |
| Most the deduction can ever be worth | about $180 |

The most generous homeschool tax deduction in the country is worth roughly
**$78 a year** at our level of spending, and about $180 to anyone who spends
$12,000 or more. We claim it. It is real, it is worth the ten minutes, and it
is not a reason to choose or reject homeschooling.

That is the honest shape of homeschool tax relief in America: one state with a
deduction worth less than a tank of fuel, and forty-five with nothing at all.
Anyone telling you the tax treatment makes homeschooling cheap is selling
something.

**Education savings accounts are the thing that actually moves money.**
Louisiana's LA GATOR program pays $5,243 per eligible student for 2026-27,
$7,626 for families at or below 250% of the federal poverty line, and up to
$15,253 for students with a documented disability. That is not a deduction, it
is a funded account — enough to cover this entire program.

Three caveats that matter more than the headline:

- **It is rationed.** Over 39,000 students applied in the program's first year
  and roughly 6,000 were funded. Eligibility does not mean an award.
- **It is incompatible with registered home study.** A GATOR participant cannot
  simultaneously be in a BESE-registered home study program, which has knock-on
  effects for state scholarship eligibility later.
- **The student has to be in Louisiana.** Domicile and residence are different
  things. This household files as Louisiana resident, which is what the
  deduction turns on, but the child is schooled overseas — and GATOR money moves
  quarterly through approved providers for education actually delivered. We have
  not claimed GATOR and do not assume we could. It is recorded here because it
  decides the arithmetic for most Louisiana families reading this, and because
  it will decide ours if we move back.

## What this page does not show

**It does not show that homeschooling is cheap.** The average American
homeschooling family spends about $1,295 a year on an elementary student,
according to a 2024 survey of more than 4,000 families by the Home School Legal
Defense Association. Our homeschool costs **four times that**. We buy an
accredited enrollment with graded transcripts, and we pay a tutoring academy
three days a week. A family doing this on library books and free curriculum
would spend a fraction of what we spend.

So the claim is not that homeschooling saves money. It is narrower and harder to
argue with: *we spend well above what the average homeschooling family spends,
and it still costs a fifth of the school down the road.*

**It does not count a parent's time.** Somebody has to be in the room. That
person is not at a job, and the wages they don't earn are the biggest cost of
homeschooling by a wide margin. No line above contains it. Most published
homeschool cost figures leave it out too, which is why they all look cheap.

A microschool charges $8,239 partly because it has to pay that person. We charge
ourselves nothing. Put your own hourly rate on it before deciding this is a
bargain.

**It is one family, one year, one country.** We are American, we live overseas,
and we are not entitled to a DoD school seat. Change any one of those and the
page comes out differently. A family in Ohio has a free school four blocks
away.

**Currency.** Won figures use **1 USD = 1,335.68 KRW**, the rate on 16 November
2026. The won moves. These numbers do not.
