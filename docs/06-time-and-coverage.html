06 — Time and coverage, measured against a public elementary benchmark

This is a time and coverage comparison. It makes no claim about learning outcomes; see Claims and limits for why not.

Core instruction hours are measured. The daily packet logs start time, finish time and total breaks, and the tracker holds every graded result.

Enrichment hours are estimated and labelled as such throughout. They are the programme owner's figures for session lengths, not logged times. Treat the core column as evidence and the enrichment column as a good-faith estimate until it is logged the same way.

The benchmark

US public elementary, grades 1–4, from the federal Schools and Staffing Survey:

Subject Hours per week
English / language arts 11.6
Mathematics 5.4
Social studies 2.5
Science 2.3
Core instruction 21.8
Student school week 32.1
Not core instruction 10.3 (32% of the week)

Nationally the average student spends about 1,231 hours in school per year, and the same body of research finds interruptions and teacher absence cost a meaningful slice of that — roughly 97 hours a year in one district study.

The number that matters is the third row: about a third of the public elementary week is not core instruction. Transitions, lining up, attendance, assemblies, and the overhead of moving 25 children through a building. None of it is wasted exactly, but none of it is instruction either.

Important caveat for honest comparison: those hours cover eight subjects — Bible, Reading, Language, Writing, Spelling, Arithmetic, History and Science — where the 21.8-hour benchmark covers four. Spelling and handwriting are separately timetabled here and are folded into English in the benchmark. The totals are close; the baskets are not identical. Read it as comparable, not equal.

The comparison

Hours per week
Public elementary, in school 32.1 — of which 21.8 is core instruction
This programme, core instruction 19.6 – 26.7
This programme, core + enrichment 34.6 – 47.2 structured

Core instruction is comparable. The difference is what fills the rest of the week: about 10 hours of non-instructional school time, against 15–20 hours of chosen academic tutoring, scouting and sport.

That is the whole claim. Not better learning — the same instructional load in a shorter day, with the recovered time spent on things that were chosen rather than inherited.

On the socialization question

The most common objection to homeschooling is social isolation. The research does not support the objection in either direction — reviewers find no evidence of help or harm, and say so because the studies are too weak to conclude anything.

What this programme can report is a schedule: roughly 7 hours a week of structured peer activity across scouting and two sports programmes, plus 3 sessions a week in a classroom with other children. Five days a week this student is in a room with other kids doing something organised.

That is not evidence about socialization outcomes. It is evidence that the scheduling objection — that a homeschooled child has no structured peer contact — does not describe this programme. Those are different claims and the weaker one is the defensible one.

Flexibility, concretely

The things the schedule can absorb that a fixed timetable cannot:

  • A subject can run at the student's actual level. Mathematics here runs well above grade level because there is nothing forcing it to the median of a class.
  • Work can move sites. The mathematics work-text is done at a tutoring centre three days a week; the home packets carry only the video lessons and the graded instruments. The split is documented so neither site duplicates or drops the other's work.
  • A bad day can be re-dated instead of lost. When a grading period slipped two weeks, it was re-baselined and brought back inside its deadline by adding one weekend make-up day — not by cutting content.
  • Assessment is placed where the student is fresh. Graded items are deliberately positioned in the packet. One quiz taken early, out of its planned slot, produced a score 10 points below the same student's rate-matched result a week earlier — visible precisely because the conditions were logged.

The last one cuts both ways and is included for that reason.
