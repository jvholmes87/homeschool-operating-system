Who we are

Two fair questions

People ask homeschooling families two things, usually politely and sometimes not. Is the child isolated? Is the parent actually qualified to teach? Here are both answers, with numbers rather than reassurance.

The student

Upper elementary · Grade 4

She is never named here

No name, no age beyond the grade, no photographs, no school identifiers, and nothing that puts her in a particular place at a particular time. Her grades and her hours are public; her identity is not. She will be an adult one day and none of this will be attached to her name when she gets there. The rules we hold ourselves to are written down and enforced by the build, not by remembering.

What she is like to teach

Fast. She reads around 175 words a minute — roughly 175% of the pace the curriculum allots for her grade — and finishes a timed reading in a little over half the time allowed. She works above grade level in mathematics, far enough that she does that workbook at a tutoring academy three days a week with a specialist rather than at our kitchen table.

She draws constantly. Asked in a scouting career exercise what she wanted to be, she wrote textile engineer, and gave two reasons:

It gives me joy.
It makes me happy I can draw my imaginations. — her answers, written at nine

And she asked, more than once, where her breaks were on the paper. She was right to. A ten-year-old who cannot see a break coming experiences the day as unbroken work, so now every break is printed as a numbered item in the running order, with its length, tickable like anything else. Most of what is good about this programme started as her telling us something was wrong with it.

Her week, in hours

Reported as hours by category — never days, times, named organisations or places, for the reasons above.

Core instruction, 5 days19.6–26.7 hrs
Academic tutoring, 3×/week (maths and a second language)9–12 hrs
Scouting, one meeting1.5–2 hrs
Sports club1.5–2 hrs
Youth sports — 2 practices + 1 game, seasonal3–4.5 hrs

Core hours are logged daily. Enrichment hours are currently the parent’s estimates of session lengths and are labelled as estimates everywhere they appear, until they are logged the same way.

On the isolation question. Five days a week she is in a room with other children doing something organised — three classroom sessions at the tutoring academy, a scout meeting, and two sports programmes. That is roughly seven hours a week of structured time with peers on top of the classroom time.

That is a claim about a calendar, not about her social development, and we are careful about the difference. The research finds no evidence of harm or benefit either way, mostly because the studies are too weak to show anything. What we can say is that the scheduling version of the objection — that a homeschooled child has no structured contact with other kids — does not describe this.

The parent

Running the programme

Jason V. Holmes

Single father with full physical custody. Ten years in the US Air Force, finishing as an NCO. Managed a $550,000 unmanned aircraft systems programme, supervised up to five operators, and led the first Air Force unit to inspect installation roofs by drone. Before that, ran installation geospatial records — as-built documentation, mapping layers, field surveys — which is where the habit of writing everything down came from.

BS, Computer Science (University of Maryland Global Campus, 2026), currently partway through an MS in IT Systems Engineering. Background in civil engineering. Day job is designing operating systems for small contractors — cutting rework, compliance misses and manual admin.

Why that background matters here, and where it doesn’t

An accredited homeschool is not really a teaching problem. It is a compliance operation that happens to involve teaching: every video lesson must be watched, every grading period ships by a fixed date, tests are opened and proctored and stored under rules, there are no retakes ever, and a parent signs a statement each period attesting that all of it was done properly. One person is simultaneously teacher, proctor, registrar and quality control.

That part is squarely programme management, and it is the part most families are given no help with. It is also the part that quietly fails — the work gets done but the evidence and the sequencing drift, and nobody notices until a deadline.

What it does not make him is a qualified elementary teacher. He isn’t one, and the design of this programme assumes it. The instruction comes from an accredited video curriculum taught by actual teachers. The tests are graded by the accrediting body, not by the parent. Mathematics is handed to a specialist three days a week. Reading speed is measured but never allowed to touch a grade.

The honest version is that the structure is built to be robust to the parent not being a teacher — which is the situation nearly every homeschooling family is actually in.

Why any of this is public

The strongest argument against homeschooling has never really been test scores. It is that it happens behind a closed door, where nobody can check. Proposals to restrict it lean on exactly that.

Arguing back with a test score does not answer it. Opening the door does. So the method, the hours, the grades and the mistakes are all here, fixed in advance, for anyone who wants to check the work.

If it helps one other family run their programme better, that is the point. If it gives one parent a straight answer at a family dinner, that is a bonus.
