Who we are

Two fair questions

People ask homeschooling families two things, usually politely and sometimes not. Is the child isolated? Is the parent actually qualified to teach? Here are both answers, with numbers rather than reassurance.

We are an American family living in South Korea, near Osan Air Base — an Air Force veteran and his daughter. That is relevant rather than incidental: a large share of the people who end up considering homeschooling are military or living overseas, for reasons that have nothing to do with ideology. More on that below, because if that is you, this site was written for you first.

The student

Upper elementary · Grade 4

She is never named here

No name, no date of birth, no age beyond the grade, no photographs, and no school identifiers. We name the region we live in, because it is the whole reason this program is shaped the way it is — but never an address, a named troop, academy or club, or a day and time she can be found at one. Place without a schedule and without a name does not locate a child; place with them does, so those stay out permanently.

Her grades and her hours are public. Her identity is not. She will be an adult one day and none of this will be attached to her name when she gets there. The rules we hold ourselves to are written down and enforced by the build rather than by remembering — publishing fails outright if her name appears in any generated page.

What she is like to teach

In third grade she read all seven Harry Potter books and the whole Diary of a Wimpy Kid series.

The measured version of that is 175 words a minute, about 175% of the pace her curriculum allows for her grade, finishing a timed reading in a little over half the time given. The number is the evidence. The books are the reason anyone should care about the number.

She works above grade level in math, far enough that she does that workbook at a tutoring academy three days a week with a specialist rather than at our kitchen table.

She draws constantly. Asked in a scouting career exercise what she wanted to be, she wrote textile engineer, and gave two reasons:

It gives me joy.
It makes me happy I can draw my imaginations. — her own answers, from a scouting career exercise

And she asked, more than once, where her breaks were on the paper. She was right to. A child who cannot see a break coming experiences the day as unbroken work, so now every break is printed as a numbered item in the running order, with its length, tickable like anything else. Most of what is good about our homeschool started as her telling us something was wrong with it.

Her week, in hours

Core hours are logged daily. Enrichment hours are currently the parent’s estimates of session lengths and are labelled as estimates everywhere they appear, until they are logged the same way.

On the isolation question. Five days a week she is in a room with other children doing something organized — three classroom sessions at the hagwon, a scout meeting, and two sports programs, one with American kids on base and one with Korean kids off it. That is roughly seven hours a week of structured time with peers on top of the classroom time, in two languages.

That is a claim about a calendar, not about her social development, and we are careful about the difference. The research finds no evidence of harm or benefit either way, mostly because the studies are too weak to show anything. What we can say is that the scheduling version of the objection — that a homeschooled child has no structured contact with other kids — does not describe this.

The parent

Running the program

Jason V. Holmes

Single father with full physical custody. Ten years in the US Air Force, finishing as an NCO, most of it in Korea. Managed a $550,000 unmanned aircraft systems program at Osan Air Base, supervised up to five operators, ran the airfield damage assessment mission, and led the first Air Force unit to inspect installation roofs by drone. Before that, ran installation geospatial records at Kunsan — as-built documentation, mapping layers, field surveys — which is where the habit of writing everything down came from.

Honorably discharged in 2023 and stayed. Thirteen years in the country now, which is the reason a Korean hagwon is a normal part of a school week here rather than an exotic detail.

BS, Computer Science (University of Maryland Global Campus, 2026), currently partway through an MS in IT Systems Engineering. Background in civil engineering. Day job is designing operating systems for small contractors — cutting rework, compliance misses and manual admin.

Why that background matters here, and where it doesn’t

An accredited homeschool combines teaching with a substantial compliance and records-management workload: every video lesson must be watched, every grading period ships by a fixed date, tests are opened and proctored and stored under rules, there are no retakes ever, and a parent signs a statement each period attesting that all of it was done properly. One person is simultaneously teacher, proctor, registrar and quality control. None of that reduces the teaching to paperwork — it sits on top of it.

That second half is squarely program management, and it is the half most families are given no help with. It is also the half that quietly fails — the teaching gets done, but the evidence and the sequencing drift, and nobody notices until a deadline.

What it does not make him is a qualified elementary teacher. He isn’t one, and the design of our homeschool assumes it. The instruction comes from an accredited video curriculum taught by actual teachers. The tests are graded by the accrediting body, not by the parent. Mathematics is handed to a specialist three days a week. Reading speed is measured but never allowed to touch a grade.

The honest version is that the structure is built to be robust to the parent not being a teacher — which is the situation nearly every homeschooling family is actually in.

If you are military, or overseas, this is for you

Homeschooling gets discussed as though everyone arrives at it the same way. Military and expatriate families mostly do not arrive at it by conviction. They arrive at it by circumstance, and often reluctantly:

  • You move mid-year. A PCS in February means a child changes schools in the middle of a curriculum, in a system that does not hand over cleanly, and loses ground that nobody ever gets around to measuring.
  • The school on base may not fit. It may not exist at your location, it may be full, it may not serve your child’s level, or the commute may eat the day.
  • Host-nation school is a bigger decision than it sounds. It can be excellent. It can also mean your child’s academic record is now in another language and another system, which matters later, at exactly the moment nobody is thinking about it.
  • Your family is already split across time zones. Grandparents, a co-parent, a specialist, a tutor — you are already running a schedule that no local school calendar was built around.
  • You will probably move again. A curriculum you carry with you is worth something that a good local school cannot offer.

An accredited correspondence program answers most of that, which is why overseas families reach for one. What it does not answer is how to actually run it when you are one adult, in a different time zone than the school, shipping graded work by international mail against fixed deadlines. That gap is what this site is about.

The base community does a lot of the work here. Scouting, youth sports, the other families — the structured peer time in the table above mostly runs through the installation, and we lean on it deliberately. If you are at an overseas base and weighing this, that infrastructure is already there and it is the part people underestimate most.

The other half runs through the local Korean community, off base. Both halves matter, and a homeschool schedule is what makes fitting both in possible.

Why any of this is public

The strongest argument against homeschooling has never really been test scores. It is that it happens behind a closed door, where nobody can check. Proposals to restrict it lean on exactly that.

Arguing back with a test score does not answer it. Opening the door does. So the method, the hours, the grades and the mistakes are all here, fixed in advance, for anyone who wants to check the work.

If it helps one other family run their program better, that is the point. If it gives one parent a straight answer at a family dinner, that is a bonus.
